# The Price of Kelingking Beach's Beauty on Nusa Penida

A few days ago, we posted our travelogue '[Solo Female Travel in Bali: 8 Days of Adventure 🌺](https://gobaligo.id/en/blog/bali-solo-female-travel/)', and many readers asked for the exact location of this stunning beach.

## **⚠️** Recent Incident: The Accident on September 12

A serious accident occurred yesterday at Nusa Penida's famous Kelingking Beach (also known as精灵沙滩). A 29-year-old Australian woman, Brianna Lani Cronin, arrived at Kelingking Beach around 8 a.m. on September 12. She then ventured alone to a cliffside area that is not part of the official trail to the beach. Local SAR reported that the route to the beach is equipped with safety ropes, but this tourist took a different path leading directly to the cliff edge, an area sometimes visited by locals for photos. She slipped and fell, ultimately landing on the beach below.

Due to Kelingking's extremely steep terrain, it took SAR about 1.5 hours to recover her body.

Open Instagram and search for 'Bali,' and about three out of ten photos will show the same scene: turquoise waters surrounding a massive cliff shaped like a T-Rex head, with a tiny traveler standing at the edge, looking no bigger than a comma. This is Kelingking Beach, the most iconic landmark on Nusa Penida and one of the most photographed beaches in the world.

But beyond the lens, this stunning beauty comes with a price—some paid in physical effort, others in real accidents. Today, I want to share the truths behind the beauty that you need to know before adding Kelingking Beach to your Bali itinerary.

## 1. Getting Down to the Beach Is More Exhausting Than You Think

https://www.instagram.com/reel/DarMTu_AcjI/?utm_source=ig_web_button_share_sheet

Many visitors just snap photos from the clifftop viewpoint and leave, but what truly sets Kelingking Beach apart is the steep, nearly vertical descent path. The entire downward trek takes about 30 to 45 minutes over loose gravel and dirt, which becomes dangerously slippery after rain. Climbing back up usually takes nearly an hour, and the physical toll is far beyond what most imagine as a 'beach stroll.'

If you plan to go all the way down, wear sturdy hiking or athletic shoes with good traction—flip-flops or sandals are practically asking for a twisted ankle on this trail. It's also wise to complete your descent and return before 12:30 p.m., as the midday sun in Bali is brutally intense, and the path offers almost no shade.

A more pressing issue is that the trail has virtually no official safety measures—no sturdy handrails, with some sections relying on flimsy ropes or wooden stakes for support. On wet or loose gravel surfaces, one misstep can lead to a slip or sprain. Every year, a significant number of tourists fall and get injured while going up or down, ranging from minor scrapes and bruises to fractures requiring evacuation. These accidents rarely make the news, but local guides say they're 'almost a daily occurrence.'

## 2. The Beach Looks Calm, but the Currents Are Deadly

This is the most overlooked yet critical point: Kelingking Beach appears serene on the surface, but the underwater currents are fierce. Facing the open Indian Ocean, it has strong rip currents, and the seabed terrain becomes complex during low tide, capable of dragging even experienced swimmers away from shore in an instant. Local authorities have already posted signs prohibiting swimming, yet there are still frequent cases of tourists ignoring warnings, being swept away by waves, getting injured, or needing rescue.

There are no lifeguards on duty, no warning flags, or rescue equipment on the beach. In other words, if an accident happens, whether you're spotted and rescued in time depends entirely on luck. If you want to take photos or wade in the sand, it's best to only approach the water during low tide and calm surf, and never turn your back on the waves.

## 3. There Used to Be 'Convenience Facilities,' but They Were Shut Down for Safety Reasons

In the past, there were commercial facilities like glass elevators and bungee jumping here, marketed as ways for visitors to enjoy the scenery without the strenuous climb or to seek more thrilling experiences. However, these facilities were ordered to halt operations or shut down by local government committees due to a lack of legal permits and failure to meet safety standards. In other words, to experience Kelingking Beach, you still have to rely on your own two feet for the entire journey—no shortcuts.

In a way, this is actually a good thing—rather than chasing fast-paced tourism development, preserving this natural landscape in its original state offers better long-term protection for the ecosystem and visitor safety.

## 4. The Price of Beauty Is Actually 'Respect'

What makes Kelingking Beach so dangerous often isn't the attraction itself, but visitors underestimating nature's temper. The waves won't go easy on you just because you're a tourist snapping Instagram-worthy photos, and the steep slopes won't become gentle and easy just because you're in a hurry.

If you're planning to go, here are some practical tips:

- Check the weather the night before; trails are especially slippery and dangerous after rain
- Let your travel companions or accommodation know your itinerary
- Bring plenty of water; there's little shade and high temperatures on the hill
- Wear shoes with good traction; don't go down the slope in flip-flops or sandals
- Never play in the water or swim at the beach—the 'no swimming' signs are not just decorations.

Bali's beauty has never been without its risks. Kelingking Beach is worth adding to your itinerary, but it's also worth taking an extra five minutes to read this article carefully—more than a perfect photo, making it safely back up the cliff is the real souvenir you should bring home from this trip.
