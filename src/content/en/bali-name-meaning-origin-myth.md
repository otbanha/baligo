# Does 'Bali' mean 'return'? Unraveling the origin of Bali's name: The cultural story of the 'Island of Offerings'

"Bali means 'return'—everyone who visits Bali will eventually come back!"

Many travelers have heard this saying.

Whenever someone asks about the origin of the name Bali, there's always someone who explains that 'Bali' means 'return' in Indonesian, so those who visit will miss the place and can't resist coming back again.

It's a romantic notion that fits Bali's enchanting image.

But here's the question: **Does 'Bali' really mean 'return' in Indonesian?**

In fact, this is a common mix-up between language and place names. From a linguistic, historical, and cultural perspective, Bali has another fascinating explanation: it may derive from ancient Sanskrit, meaning 'offering, tribute, or sacrifice.'

This article takes you through the history and culture behind the name Bali.

## 1. Does "Bali" Mean "Return"?

First, let's start with the language.

In Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia), common words for "return" or "come back" include:

- **Kembali**: to return, come back.
- **Balik**: to go back, return, also meaning to flip or turn over.

So why do some people say Bali means "return"?

The reason likely relates to Javanese (Bahasa Jawa).

In Javanese, **bali does indeed mean to go home or return**. The Indonesian Ministry of Education's Javanese-Indonesian Dictionary also lists this word, defining it as pulang (to go home).

Thus, Bali is not entirely unrelated to the concept of "return" linguistically, but a distinction must be made:

- Javanese bali: to go home, return.
- In Indonesian, "kembali" means to return or come back.
- Bali: a place name with a long history.

These three cannot be directly equated.

In other words, while Javanese has a word "bali" meaning "to return," it doesn't mean the name of this Indonesian island evolved from the concept of "coming back."

This is also why this explanation is easily spread as misinformation online.

## 2. Another Interpretation of Bali: Derived from Ancient Sanskrit, Meaning Offering and Devotion

![raw-image](https://images.gobaligo.id/images/2026-11/1795704297808-15902.png)

Aside from the "return" theory, there's another noteworthy etymological explanation for Bali: its roots in ancient Sanskrit.

In Sanskrit:

**Bali (बलि)**

carries meanings such as offering, sacrifice, ritual tribute, and devotion.

And another Sanskrit term:

**Dwipa (द्वीप)**

means island.

Thus, Bali Dwipa (Bali Dvipa) has a common etymological interpretation:

**Island of Offerings.**

This explanation is not merely a tourist marketing story. A 2023 academic study exploring the place names of Bali proposed that Bali Dwipa may derive from the Sanskrit bali-dvīpa, meaning 'Island of Offerings.'

However, it should be noted that the exact etymology of Bali still has other scholarly interpretations, so 'Island of Offerings' should be regarded as a well-supported etymological hypothesis rather than a fully settled translation.

## 3. Why Does 'Island of Offerings' Fit Balinese Culture So Well?

If you've ever been to Bali, you're likely familiar with the religious offerings that are ubiquitous across the island.

Whether at hotel entrances, restaurants, shops, homes, or even on streets and in front of temples, you'll often see small woven bamboo leaf baskets filled with flowers, rice grains, food, and incense.

This is a very important part of daily life in Bali—the culture of religious offerings.

### Canang Sari: Offerings You See Every Day

![raw-image](https://images.gobaligo.id/images/2026-11/1795704359052-15900.webp)

Canang Sari are small daily offerings made by Balinese Hindus.

They are typically crafted from woven coconut or palm leaves into small baskets, filled with colorful flowers, grains of rice, and other offerings, sometimes accompanied by incense.

These offerings are often placed at home entrances, shops, temples, and other locations as a gesture of gratitude and respect to the gods.

For locals, this is not just a religious ritual but an integral part of everyday life.

### Banten: Offerings in Various Religious Ceremonies

Banten is a general term for the various offerings used in Balinese Hindu rituals.

Depending on the specific ceremony, occasion, and purpose, the materials, shapes, and methods of making these offerings vary.

Some offerings are quite intricate, made with fresh flowers, fruits, rice, coconut leaves, and other materials, showcasing Bali’s unique religious art and culture.

### Yadnya: The Spirit of Offering and Sacrifice

Yadnya is a key concept of offerings and sacrifice in Balinese Hindu culture.

It represents not just the act of giving items, but also a spirit of gratitude and devotion to gods, ancestors, nature, and life.

From daily small offerings to grand temple ceremonies, this cultural importance is evident throughout life in Bali.

Therefore, when we see that Bali may be related to the Sanskrit word Bali (meaning offering, tribute, or sacrifice), it’s easy to understand why the interpretation of the 'Island of Offerings' resonates so deeply with Bali’s cultural identity.

## 4. How Ancient Is the Name Bali? Searching for Answers in Sanur’s Millennia-Old Inscription

![raw-image](https://images.gobaligo.id/images/2026-11/1795704722362-15906.jpg)

To understand the history of Bali’s name, one must mention the Blanjong Inscription located in Sanur.

This ancient stone pillar, known as **Prasasti Blanjong (Blanjong Inscription)**, is a very important historical artifact of Bali.

The inscription is associated with King Sri Kesari Warmadewa, who ruled Bali at the time, dating to the Saka year 835, which is usually converted to around 913 AD.

The inscription features the name **Walidwipa**, which is generally believed to refer to present-day Bali.

Where:

- Wali/Bali: part of the place name.
- Dwipa: Sanskrit for 'island'.

The significance of this stone lies in the fact that it provides tangible evidence of Bali's early place names and history.

It's worth noting that the Walidwipa in the inscription doesn't directly prove that Bali derives from the Sanskrit word for 'offering' or 'sacrifice', but it does show that the name of Bali has a very long history, far from being a modern tourist invention.

If you have the chance to visit Sanur, consider heading to the Blanjong area to see this ancient stone that bears witness to Bali's thousand-year history.

## 5. Is Bali Really the 'Island of Offerings' or Something Else?

In fact, there is no fully consistent academic consensus on the exact etymology of Bali.

Beyond the Sanskrit interpretation of *bali-dvīpa* (Island of Offerings), another theory suggests that Bali may be related to the Sanskrit word *Vali* (वली).

This interpretation understands Bali Dwipa as a name connected to waves—essentially, the 'Island of Waves.'

Therefore, the current rigorous summary is as follows:

| Term | Explanation | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Javanese 'bali' | To return, to go back | Exists, but cannot directly serve as the island's name origin |
| Sanskrit 'bali' | Offering, tribute, devotion | An etymological hypothesis supported by dictionaries and toponymic research |
| Sanskrit 'vali' | Wave-related interpretation | Another etymological hypothesis |
| Bali Dwipa | Bali, the island's name | Found in ancient inscriptions and historical documents |
