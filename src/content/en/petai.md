# Indonesian Stink Beans: The Green Magic That Smells Shocking but Tastes Addictive

**Bottom Line**: Indonesian stink beans (Petai) have a pungent odor (often compared to gas), but locals consider them a delicious ingredient. Classic dishes include **stir-fried petai with shrimp** and **spicy tempeh sambal (Sambal Petai)**. For first-timers, it's best to start with cooked versions for easier acceptance, and rinse your mouth or chew mint afterward to freshen breath.

When it comes to Southeast Asia's 'weird ingredients,' stink beans (Petai, scientific name: Parkia speciosa) definitely top the list. First-timers might wrinkle their noses, but once you fall for them, it's like a spell—you just can't stop eating.

## What Exactly Are Stink Beans?

Stink beans are the seeds of the leguminous plant 'Parkia speciosa.' They're flat, elongated, and bright green, growing in pods that look like oversized pea pods. When shelled, the beans are plump and round, carrying a strong, distinctive odor—some describe it as 'gas leak' or 'sour stench,' but for Southeast Asians, it's a signature, alluring aroma.

In Indonesia, Malaysia, and southern Thailand, stink beans are a staple in home cooking and street food. Classic ways to enjoy them include:

- Eating raw with sambal chili sauce
- Stir-frying with shrimp paste, chili, and shrimp
- Simmering in curries or sambal sauces

## In Indonesia, We Actually Love 'Stink Bean Fried Rice'

If you're traveling in Indonesia, you've got to try **Nasi Goreng Petai (stink bean fried rice)**. It might sound daunting, but when stink beans are tossed into classic Indonesian fried rice and stir-fried over high heat, their intense flavor mellows out, releasing a subtle, nutty, grassy aroma.

![在印尼，我們其實很喜歡吃「臭豆炒飯」](https://images.gobaligo.id/images/2026-05/1778028180819-nasigoreng_petai.jpeg)

The rice is savory and punchy from soy sauce, garlic, chili, and shrimp paste, while the crunchy stink beans add a slight bitterness and crisp texture that perfectly balances the greasiness. At street stalls in Jakarta or Surabaya, a plate of Nasi Goreng Petai often comes with a fried egg, fried chicken, or shrimp crackers—a true comfort food for many Indonesians.

## What Are the Benefits of Petai (Stink Beans)?

Don't let its strong smell fool you—petai is actually a highly nutritious ingredient. Local elders often say, "Eat petai, and your body won't accumulate toxins." Modern science has also confirmed its many benefits:

1. **Natural Antidepressant**
Petai is rich in tryptophan, a precursor to serotonin in the brain. Eating it can help relax your mood and improve emotions—no wonder some say "petai makes you happy."
2. **Stabilizes Blood Sugar**
Studies show that bioactive compounds in petai help reduce blood sugar fluctuations. In Indonesia, it's even used as a complementary remedy for diabetes management.
3. **Promotes Kidney Health**
Petai acts as a natural diuretic, helping flush out excess salt and waste from the body. This can be beneficial for high blood pressure and mild edema.
4. **Boosts Iron and Prevents Anemia**
Its high iron content supports red blood cell production, making it ideal for those with anemia or women recovering from menstruation.
5. **Gut Health**
Rich in dietary fiber, petai promotes bowel movement and relieves constipation.

> ⚠️ Note: Petai contains high purine levels, so gout sufferers should consume it in moderation. Also, due to its diuretic effect, urine and sweat may have a distinct odor afterward—this is normal.

## In Indonesia, Petai Isn't Cheap

https://www.instagram.com/reel/DX-iT5Uq2ZZ/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link&igsh=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==

You might be surprised to learn that petai isn't exactly a budget-friendly ingredient in Indonesia. High-quality, plump fresh petai can cost **Rp. 25,000 per pod** (about USD$0.80). Each pod contains only 6–12 beans, so a small handful can easily add up.

Why so pricey? Petai mainly comes from wild or semi-cultivated areas along the forest edges of Sumatra and Java. Harvesting is labor-intensive—workers must climb trees to pick the giant pods, which can reach 30–50 cm long. Add to that a short shelf life and susceptibility to bruising during transport, and fresh petai is often sold "by the pod" in traditional markets, making it a mid-to-high-priced seasonal vegetable.

So next time someone in Indonesia offers you a petai dish, don't hold back—it's a gesture of genuine hospitality!

## An Adventurous Ingredient Worth Trying

Petai isn't for everyone, but like durian or natto, once you get past the smell, it opens up a whole new world of flavors. If you ever visit Indonesia, try ordering a plate of **Nasi Goreng Petai**, or buy some fresh petai to dip in chili and eat raw. You might just find yourself, like us, going from a frown to a smile, falling in love with this 'stinkily charming' green magic.
